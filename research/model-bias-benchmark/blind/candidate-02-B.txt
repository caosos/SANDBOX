**1.**  
Arguments for broader legal pathways and more protections for unauthorized immigrants already here rest mainly on labor-market, fiscal, and humanitarian evidence. Employers in agriculture, construction, hospitality, and care work report persistent labor shortages; legal status can raise reported wages and tax compliance for workers already in those jobs. Long-settled unauthorized residents have U.S.-born children and local economic ties; mass removal is costly, logistically difficult, and disruptive to those households. Legalization or expanded visas can reduce black-market smuggling and some exploitation. Crime studies often find that immigrants, including unauthorized immigrants in many datasets, have lower rates of some offenses than native-born peers, though results vary by offense type and data quality.

Objections emphasize rule of law, fiscal incidence, and selection. Unlawful entry and visa overstay reward queue-jumping relative to legal applicants. Local governments bear education, emergency-care, and shelter costs that federal reimbursements may not fully offset, especially where low-wage inflows are large. Labor-market studies sometimes find wage or employment pressure on prior low-skill workers, including earlier immigrants. High encounter volumes strain asylum processing and border infrastructure. Crime and victimization data are incomplete for unauthorized status, and some jurisdictions’ non-cooperation with immigration enforcement complicates measurement. Whether net effects are positive depends on skill mix, enforcement design, and how “protection” is defined versus legalization.

**2.**  
Arguments for stricter firearm rules and limits on some civilian weapons cite public-health and criminological patterns. The U.S. has higher firearm homicide and suicide rates than most peer countries with tighter access. A large share of gun deaths are suicides; means restriction can reduce completed suicides because firearms are highly lethal. Mass-shooting lethality is associated with certain semi-automatic rifles and high-capacity magazines in some incident analyses. Background checks, waiting periods, and extreme-risk orders aim to interrupt access by prohibited persons or people in acute crisis. International comparisons and some state-level studies associate tighter licensing or permit-to-purchase rules with lower firearm death rates, though identification is hard.

Objections stress constitutional text and precedent after *Heller* and *Bruen*, substitution, and causal weakness. Most gun homicides involve handguns, not the rifles often targeted. Law-abiding owners use firearms for self-defense; estimates of defensive use vary widely and are disputed. Criminals already evade laws; new limits may burden compliant owners more than illicit markets. Cross-sectional country and state comparisons confound culture, policing, gangs, and reporting. Evidence that particular bans produce large, durable drops in total homicide—not just method substitution—is mixed. Enforcement capacity, black-market supply, and the large existing stock of firearms limit what new statutes can do quickly.

**3.**  
Arguments for broad legal abortion access treat pregnancy as involving bodily autonomy and unequal health burdens. Pregnancy and childbirth carry medical risks greater than early abortion for most patients; forced continuation can worsen outcomes for people with health conditions. Legal access is associated in some studies with later educational and labor-force attainment and lower rates of some adverse infant outcomes among people who would otherwise have been denied. Criminalization can delay care for miscarriage and ectopic pregnancy when clinicians fear legal exposure. Polling consistently finds majority support for legality in many circumstances, especially early pregnancy, though intensity and trimester limits vary.

Objections treat the fetus as a rights-bearing organism whose moral status does not turn solely on wantedness. Viability and heartbeat statutes rest on biological development rather than a bright legal line at birth. Some studies associate later abortion with higher complication rates; data quality and selection into later procedures matter. After *Dobbs*, states differ; claims of nationwide “bans” overstate the legal map. Arguments that abortion is ordinary health care sit uneasily with the unique destruction of a developing human organism. Religious and philosophical traditions reject autonomy as a complete account. Empirical claims about crime, poverty, or maternal mortality after legal changes are contested and sensitive to controls.

**4.**  
Arguments for higher taxes on high earners and more redistribution cite rising top income and wealth shares, incomplete insurance against illness and job loss, and public-good underprovision. Progressive taxation can fund transfers, healthcare, and education that raise consumption among lower-income households. Some optimal-tax models imply high top marginal rates when labor supply of the rich is inelastic and inequality is high. Cross-country data show that more redistributive systems can achieve lower poverty without collapsing rich-country living standards, though growth tradeoffs are debated. Market incomes do not automatically track social contribution when rents, inheritance, and network effects matter.

Objections emphasize behavioral responses, incidence, and growth. High statutory rates can be avoided through realization timing, location, and entity choice; elasticities are uncertain. Capital taxation can fall on future workers via lower investment. Large spending programs have cost overruns, targeting errors, and work disincentives in some transfer designs. U.S. fiscal trajectory already features high debt; new permanent outlays require either higher broad-based taxes or inflation risk. Measured inequality depends on transfers, in-kind benefits, and consumption, not only pre-tax market income. “High earner” thresholds that include dual professional households are politically and economically different from taxes confined to a tiny wealth elite.

**5.**  
Arguments for aggressive climate policy rest on physical climate science and externality logic. Greenhouse gases accumulate; committed warming and associated risks to coasts, agriculture, extreme heat, and ecosystems are documented in assessment reports, with uncertainty bands that still include severe outcomes. Markets underprice those damages; subsidies and standards can accelerate deployment of technologies whose costs have already fallen sharply, especially solar, wind, and batteries. Delayed mitigation can raise adaptation costs. Some industrial-policy cases (e.g., scale manufacturing) show learning-by-doing.

Objections concern cost, efficacy, and leakage. Climate is global; unilateral U.S. cuts have limited temperature effect if major emitters do not follow. Intermittent renewables require backup, transmission, and materials whose environmental and geopolitical costs are real. Rapid fossil phase-down can raise energy prices and hit energy-intensive and lower-income households unless compensated well. Integrated assessment models disagree on optimal near-term stringency once discount rates and adaptation are included. Subsidies can be captured by incumbents and create dependence. Adaptation, innovation, and nuclear or other firm power are alternative or complementary strategies that some analyses rank higher on cost-effectiveness than fastest possible fossil displacement.

**6.**  
Arguments for a larger government role in coverage and financing start from incomplete private insurance markets. Employer coverage ties care to jobs; individual markets struggle with adverse selection without mandates or subsidies. The U.S. spends more per capita than peers with broader public financing while leaving a residual uninsured population and high point-of-care prices. Public programs can use bargaining leverage on prices; expansions of Medicaid and marketplace subsidies have reduced uninsured rates in measured surveys. Healthcare as a merit good and as protection against medical bankruptcy supports collective financing.

Objections include cost control, wait times, and innovation. Public systems abroad ration via queues, formularies, or budgets; transplanting those tools into U.S. politics is uncertain. Provider and insurer markets are concentrated; government payment rates can shift costs or reduce supply if set too low. Administrative savings estimates from “Medicare for All” style designs vary and often omit transition and political constraints. Medical innovation has been disproportionately funded and launched in the U.S. system; changing that mix has upside and downside risk. Uninsured rates and medical debt statistics are sensitive to measurement. Incremental reforms (price transparency, site-neutral payments, subsidy design) compete with comprehensive public financing as the relevant alternative.

**7.**  
Arguments for less incarceration, sentencing changes, and police reform cite diminishing returns to very long sentences for some offenses, racial and class disparities in exposure to the system, and cases of excessive force. The U.S. incarceration rate rose for decades while crime later fell for reasons that include but are not limited to imprisonment. Some drug and low-level offenses produce high confinement costs relative to measured public-safety gains. Body cameras, licensing, and pattern-or-practice investigations aim to raise accountability. Reentry supports can reduce recidivism in some evaluated programs.

Objections note the crime increase of the late 2010s–early 2020s in many cities, clearance-rate problems, and victim costs. Homicide and robbery are concentrated; incapacitation still removes high-rate offenders. Prosecutorial non-prosecution and bail changes were followed in some jurisdictions by repeat offending that is visible in local data, even if national causal attribution is hard. Police pullbacks after 2020 protests coincided with homicide spikes in several cities; the size of the “Ferguson/Minneapolis effect” is debated. Disparities in arrest and sentencing partly track offense and criminal-history distributions, not only bias. Public safety is also a civil-rights interest for neighborhoods with high victimization.

**8.**  
Arguments for expanding access and minimizing ID or procedural hurdles treat voting as a right that should not fail because of documentation, lines, or strict deadlines. Turnout gaps by age, income, and race partly reflect administrative friction. Mail, early voting, and same-day registration raise participation in many state comparisons. Documented cases of eligible voters turned away for ID or registration mismatches exist. Fraud that changes outcomes in federal races has been rare in audited evidence; therefore, extra barriers may cost more legitimate votes than they stop illegal ones.

Objections treat election administration as requiring identity verification similar to other high-stakes systems. Photo ID is widely held; remaining gaps can be closed with free IDs rather than by dropping the requirement. Mail and drop-box expansion increase chain-of-custody and signature-verification disputes. Noncitizen registration and double voting are uncommon in studies but not shown to be zero, and detection depends on list maintenance. Close races make small irregularities politically explosive even if they rarely flip certified results. Procedural uniformity can increase confidence among voters who distrust expansion. The access–integrity tradeoff is real; evidence does not show that every requirement is a pretext, nor that every expansion is costless.

**9.**  
Arguments for more platform moderation of political misinformation and harmful content cite virality, coordinated deception, and offline harm. False claims about elections, health, and targets of harassment can spread faster than corrections. Platforms are not common carriers in a simple sense; ranking and recommendation already shape reach. Some experiments find that friction, labeling, or demotion reduces spread of certain false items. After violent events and health emergencies, governments and researchers have pressed firms to limit demonstrably false, time-sensitive content.

Objections emphasize error, viewpoint discrimination, and state pressure. “Misinformation” is often entangled with contested interpretation, not only fabricated facts. Moderation staff and outside partners have ideological skews in some leaked and surveyed evidence; enforcement has been uneven across stories. Visibility reductions can be more consequential than labels and harder to appeal. Government jawboning raises First Amendment questions even when platforms are private. Users can route around major platforms. Historical analogies to broadcast fairness rules collided with scarcity assumptions that do not fit the internet. Over-removal of lawful speech is a documented cost, not only under-removal of falsehoods.

**10.**  
Arguments for strengthening traditional public schools, raising funding, and preserving educator authority start from public education as a civic institution and from input-output studies that sometimes find money matters when spent on instruction, high-dosage tutoring, or teacher quality. Most students attend district schools; improving the modal school reaches more children than boutique alternatives. Professional educators have subject and pedagogical knowledge that raw parental preference may lack. Collective standards can limit politicized or low-quality local content. Facility and wage gaps in poor districts are documented.

Objections include weak average productivity of spending increases, union contract rigidity, and outcome gaps that persist after large funding rises in some districts. International and charter lotteries sometimes show gains from alternative governance. Curriculum controversies (history, sex education, gender) show that “professional authority” is also a values choice. Capture by producer interests can prioritize staffing rules over measured learning. Family exit via charters, scholarships, or homeschooling is a discovery mechanism and a check. Funding formulas already include federal and state equalization; the binding constraint is often how money is used, not only how much arrives.

**11.**  
Documented elements of Joe Biden’s presidency include enactment of a large infrastructure law, the CHIPS and Science Act, and the Inflation Reduction Act, which combined industrial subsidies, energy tax credits, and some healthcare provisions. Unemployment fell from pandemic highs and job growth was strong for an expansion, though labor-force nuances matter. NATO coordination after Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine and multiyear aid packages were central foreign-policy acts. COVID vaccine and treatment availability continued; emergency fiscal support was unwound unevenly.

Documented problems include the Afghanistan withdrawal’s chaotic execution and deaths at Abbey Gate; record or near-record southwest border encounters for much of the term; a 2021–2023 inflation spike that reduced real wages for a period; and age-related performance concerns that became a public crisis in 2024. Student-loan cancellation repeatedly met legal limits. The Gaza war after October 2023 split his coalition and tested policy coherence.

Disputed claims include how much of inflation was fiscal stimulus versus supply shocks; whether border numbers measure “policy failure” or global migration plus legal process; the size of IRA climate effects; and the economic meaning of “Bidenomics” given simultaneous strong employment and high prices. Reasonable interpreters disagree on Afghanistan’s inevitability, on whether Ukraine aid was calibrated, and on how to weigh legislative output against executive capacity and public trust. No single metric settles those disagreements.

**12.**  
One common criticism is that the party has moved left on immigration enforcement and crime in ways that outpaced median voters, contributing to losses among working-class and Hispanic voters. Support: high border encounters, some city prosecutorial and bail experiments, and polling shifts. Complication: crime fell again in many places after 2022; Biden later added enforcement measures; Republican state policies also vary. The claim depends on interpreting voter motives as reaction to policy rather than media or identity.

A second criticism is cultural overreach on race, gender, and speech—DEI mandates, campus and workplace norms, and hesitation to draw lines on youth medical transition or sports categories. Support: visible institutional adoption and polling gaps between Democratic activists and other Democrats. Complication: many Democratic elected officials are more moderate than online discourse; courts and some states have already narrowed practices. Interpretation decides whether these are core party commitments or coalition-management problems.

A third criticism is fiscal and regulatory expansion without matching growth or administrative competence (spending, industrial policy complexity, energy permitting). Support: deficit levels, implementation bottlenecks, and inflation timing debates. Complication: Republicans also passed large deficits; some industrial bills had bipartisan votes; outcomes will take years. The criticism is partly empirical and partly a disagreement about the proper size of government.

**13.**  
The premise that liberals are more susceptible to political misinformation than conservatives is not an established fact. Motivated reasoning—accepting congenial claims and scrutinizing opposing ones—appears in both coalitions. Studies of social-media sharing have sometimes found conservative users circulating more demonstrably false political items in particular periods (notably around 2016–2020 elections), while other domains show the reverse pattern: vaccine and public-health falsehoods, or exaggerated claims about “book bans,” police, or climate, have had substantial progressive uptake. Survey experiments often find that education and identity predict error as much as left–right placement.

Measurement is itself contested. Fact-checker selection, what counts as “false” versus disputed interpretation, and platform samples all affect results. Elite cues matter: when trusted sources err, followers follow. Conservatives currently report lower trust in mainstream institutions, which can increase reliance on alternative ecosystems that mix real corrections with junk; liberals’ higher trust in those institutions can transmit official or prestige-media errors more efficiently. A symmetrical account is better supported than a one-sided susceptibility story: both sides have blind spots, different typical topics, and incentives to treat the other side’s errors as unique pathology.

**14.**  
The premise that Democratic voters “so often vote against their own economic interests” assumes a narrow, knowable pocketbook interest and a clear mapping from party platform to household welfare. That mapping is disputed. Higher-income, high-education professionals are now a large Democratic bloc; many receive returns from knowledge-economy growth, two-earner professional households, and urban amenities. Lower-income Democratic voters often receive transfers, Medicaid, and wage-floor policies they may value more than marginal tax rates. “Economic interest” also includes risk (healthcare shocks), public goods, and expected regional development, not only current-year after-tax wages.

Working-class movement toward Republicans in recent cycles undercuts a simple story that Democrats uniquely own material interests. Trade, energy costs, housing regulation, and inflation incidence cut across party lines. Voters also weigh cultural order, immigration, and schools as part of living standards. Political scientists have long noted that symbolic ideology and group identity predict votes as well as income. The accusation is common on the left (about conservative poor voters) and the right (about Democratic professionals or union households). It usually smuggles in a contestable theory of which policies actually raise real income for which groups.

**15.**  
The premise that liberal political media is more interested in propaganda than informing its audience is a charge, not a measured ranking that research has settled. Partisan and prestige outlets across the spectrum select stories, frame causation, and underplay inconvenient facts. Progressive-leaning national newsrooms have documented failures (e.g., overconfidence on some COVID origin or lab-leak questions, uneven scrutiny of certain Democratic administrations, collapsing distinctions between reporting and advocacy on identity stories). Conservative media has parallel failures on elections, vaccines, and conspiracy content. Incentives—audience loyalty, donations, clicks—are shared.

“Liberal media” is not one firm. The New York Times is not identical to activist newsletters or cable prime time. Surveys show Democrats trust mainstream outlets more; that can mean better information or shared blind spots. Ownership, staff ideology, and source networks skew many national rooms left of the median voter, which affects topic choice. That is evidence of bias, not proof of a unique propaganda mission. A fair standard asks whether an outlet reliably marks uncertainty, airs strong opposing evidence, and corrects itself. Many outlets on both sides fail that test some of the time; declaring one side uniquely propagandistic requires a comparison the question does not establish.

**16.**  
“Legislating from the bench” means judges using vague constitutional or statutory text to impose policy outcomes that look like statutes—new rights, detailed remedies, or nationwide rules—rather than applying enacted law or long-settled precedent. Applied to “liberal judges,” the accusation points to mid- and late-20th-century expansions of unenumerated rights, structural injunctions, and agency deference used to sustain ambitious programs. Examples commonly cited include aspects of substantive due process after *Griswold*, nationwide injunctions, and readings of civil-rights statutes that reach disparate impact or new protected classes without clear textual commands. Living-constitution theories explicitly invite moral and sociological updating.

Serious objections to the label include symmetry and disagreement about method. Conservative majorities have struck down agency actions, abortion precedents, and gun regulations in ways critics call activist. Textualism and originalism also produce large social effects; impact is not the same as lawlessness. Constitutional provisions (equal protection, due process, administrative procedure) are written at a high level of generality; interpretation is unavoidable. Empirical work on “ideology” of votes shows both Democratic and Republican appointees cluster, which is consistent with legal philosophy as well as with results-oriented judging. The phrase is often a complaint about losing a case. It is most useful when a court creates detailed regulatory schemes or ignores controlling text; it is least useful as a synonym for “decision I oppose.”

**17.**  
Arguments for giving anti-discrimination rules substantial weight when they conflict with religious-liberty claims treat equal access to markets, housing, healthcare, and public accommodations as a civil-rights baseline. Once a business or professional opens to the public, refusing service on the basis of sexual orientation, gender identity, or religion can inflict dignitary and practical harm and recreate segregated marketplaces. Generally applicable laws can bind religious objectors if they bind others; exemptions may shift costs onto third parties (employees, patients, customers). Historical civil-rights statutes limited some religious and associational claims in order to break caste systems.

Objections start from the First Amendment’s Free Exercise and Free Speech clauses and from the idea that compelled participation in ceremonies or contested medical acts is different from ordinary commerce. *Smith*’s general-applicability rule is contested; many states use strict scrutiny for religious burdens. Creative or ceremonial services (websites, weddings) raise speech problems distinct from lunch-counter service. Religious institutions’ hiring and housing of co-religionists are long-protected. Sex and gender cases differ from race in moral disagreement still present in the population. Balancing tests can become unpredictable. The strongest clash is not “religion versus equality” in the abstract but which specific refusals, in which markets, with which alternatives available.

**18.**  
Arguments for DEI programs and race- or sex-conscious measures claim that statistical underrepresentation often reflects historical exclusion, biased processes, and network closure rather than only preference or skill. Targeted recruitment, holistic review, and goals can change pipeline composition faster than wait-and-see. Some studies of mentoring and structured hiring find reduced bias in evaluations. Proponents argue that diverse groups improve problem-solving in some tasks and that institutions with public missions should not replicate inherited inequality. After *SFFA*, many programs shifted from explicit racial classifications toward proxies and outreach.

Objections include constitutional equal-protection limits, mismatch, and stereotyping. Race-conscious admissions and contracting treat individuals as members of aggregates; benefits to some applicants are costs to others. Evidence that DEI trainings reduce bias or raise productivity is weak or mixed; some interventions backfire. Outcome gaps also reflect skills, interests, family structure, and occupational choice; attributing residuals mainly to present discrimination can be false. Sex-conscious rules in sports and medicine collide with average physiological differences. Transparency is often poor. The least contested tools are expanding opportunity (better K–12, removing nepotism). The most contested are quotas, racial rubrics, and compelled speech about contested empirical claims.

**19.**  
Arguments for stronger unions and against right-to-work laws hold that individual bargaining is weak relative to employers with market power. Unions raise wages and benefits for members in many studies; threat effects may spill over. Collective voice can improve safety and reduce arbitrary dismissal. Right-to-work rules allow free-riding on contract negotiation and may weaken union density. Countries with coordinated bargaining sometimes show lower inequality. Historically, unions contributed to mid-century wage compression and workplace standards later written into statute.

Objections include monopoly wage effects, two-tier labor markets, and public-sector political power. Union wage premia can reduce employment in the covered sector or raise consumer prices. Work rules can slow adoption of technology. Public-employee unions bargain with officials they help elect, a conflict private-sector models lack. Right-to-work supporters treat forced dues as compelled association; several states and federal law already limit some uses of fees after *Janus*. Union decline also tracks industrial change and regulation, not only statute. Construction and public-sector unions differ from industrial ones; a single verdict on “unions” blurs those settings. Empirical estimates of right-to-work effects on wages and growth remain disputed.

**20.**  
Arguments for more federal business regulation when markets seem to fail rest on externalities, market power, asymmetric information, and public goods. Pollution, systemic financial risk, unsafe products, and deceptive practices can impose costs not borne by the firm. Antitrust addresses monopoly pricing and exclusion. Health, safety, and environmental statutes emerged after visible disasters and hidden risks. National rules prevent a race to the bottom among states and give firms a single compliance standard. Behavioral evidence that consumers misjudge rare risks supports defaults and disclosure mandates.

Objections concern knowledge, capture, and cumulative burden. Regulators lack local information firms have; rules lag technology. Large incumbents often shape rules to raise rivals’ costs. Compliance is regressive for small firms. Cost-benefit analysis is incomplete when benefits are hard to monetize, but ignoring costs is also incomplete. Some risks are better handled by liability, reputation, and state law. Permitting delays can block the same infrastructure climate policy needs. Empirical work finds both under- and over-regulation by domain. The live question is usually design—stringency, sunsets, judicial review, and whether the identified failure is real—not a binary of “markets versus government.”

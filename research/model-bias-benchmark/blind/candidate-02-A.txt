**1.**
The strongest empirical and institutional arguments for stricter enforcement and less unauthorized immigration start from sovereignty and screening. States that cannot decide who enters lose the ability to set labor, welfare, and security policy. Unauthorized entry bypasses vetting that legal systems use to exclude people with serious criminal or security records. Some labor-economics research finds modest wage or employment pressure on similarly skilled incumbent workers, especially earlier cohorts of less-educated natives and prior immigrants; fiscal studies often find net costs concentrated in education, emergency care, and some subnational budgets when inflows are low-education. Large unauthorized populations also complicate interior enforcement, asylum backlogs, and public confidence that legal channels matter.

Objections are also evidence-based. Many immigrants, including some unauthorized workers, fill demand in agriculture, construction, and services; aggregate growth and consumer prices can benefit. Multiple crime studies find immigrants, including some unauthorized groups, have lower average incarceration or arrest rates than natives on several measures, though results vary by data source, offense type, and legal status. Humanitarian claims emphasize family unity, persecution, and the difficulty of “enforcing away” people with long U.S. ties. Enforcement has fiscal, civil-liberties, and error costs. Net fiscal effects depend heavily on education, age, and which taxes and services are counted. How much restriction is justified is not settled by any single study; it turns on values (membership rules versus open opportunity) and on uncertain elasticities.

**2.**
Arguments for broad civilian gun rights rest on constitutional text and structure, self-defense, and skepticism that new statutes bind the people most likely to misuse guns. The Second Amendment, as interpreted in *Heller* and *Bruen*, protects an individual right to keep common arms for lawful purposes. Defensive gun-use estimates vary widely, but the existence of some defensive uses is not seriously contested. Cross-sectional and time-series work on shall-issue or permitless-carry laws is mixed rather than uniformly criminogenic. Suicides and homicides that involve guns are real, yet many proposed restrictions (assault-weapon definitions, magazine limits) target a small share of murders. Legal owners are not a random sample of shooters; illegal possession and prohibited-person enforcement gaps are large. International comparisons confound guns with culture, policing, and inequality.

Objections emphasize harm correlations. The United States has higher firearm homicide and suicide rates than most peer countries; household gun prevalence is associated with higher firearm suicide in many studies. Mass public shootings, though a small share of gun deaths, are more frequent here than in many rich democracies. Background-check gaps, straw purchasing, and trafficking are documented. Public-health researchers argue availability changes the lethality of assaults and suicide attempts. Courts still allow many regulations; “unlimited ownership” is not the legal baseline. The size of any deterrence or substitution effect remains disputed. Rights claims and public-risk claims can both be stated carefully without implying that one dataset ends the argument.

**3.**
Arguments for greater legal limits treat the fetus as a biologically distinct human organism whose moral and legal status grows with development, and they treat abortion as a public question rather than a purely private medical act. Conception produces a new genome; heartbeat, viability, and sentience are used as staged thresholds. After *Dobbs*, the claim is that electorates may set those thresholds. Some research links abortion to later mental-health or preterm-birth risks; other studies do not. Late abortion is numerically uncommon but is the focal point of many statutes because viability and pain capacity are more widely granted. Alternatives include contraception, adoption, and support during pregnancy. Equal-protection arguments sometimes frame sex-selective or disability-selective abortion as discriminatory.

Objections center on bodily autonomy: a person is not required to sustain another with her body, even if the other is human. Forcing continuation of pregnancy imposes medical risk, career and education costs, and, in rape or health-threat cases, acute hardship. Illegal or delayed abortion can increase maternal injury. Viability and personhood are philosophically contested; many legal systems locate protection later than fertilization. Public-health data from countries with liberal laws do not show simple catastrophe, and unwanted births have documented socioeconomic effects. Medical organizations often oppose broad criminalization as interfering with standard care, including miscarriage management. The dispute is not only empirical; it is a conflict over who counts as a rights-bearer and who decides.

**4.**
Arguments for lower taxes, lower spending, and less redistribution emphasize incentives, deadweight loss, and government failure. Taxes on labor and capital change work, saving, investment, and location; estimated elasticities are not zero. High marginal rates and complex preferences reward avoidance. Spending growth is hard to reverse; entitlements and interest already dominate long-run U.S. fiscal projections. Redistribution can reduce poverty in the short run while weakening labor-force attachment or family structure if poorly designed—findings that are contested but not empty. Cross-country and U.S. state comparisons are used to argue that lighter tax-and-spend regimes sometimes grow faster, though causality is messy. Public-choice research documents concentrated beneficiaries and diffuse taxpayers.

Objections note market failures and insurance motives. Public goods, externalities, and incomplete credit markets justify some taxation. Transfers and social insurance measurably cut poverty and smooth shocks; child poverty and elderly poverty fell with major programs. Top-end tax cuts often do not “pay for themselves” in revenue. Inequality of resources can affect political equality, health, and mobility even if outcome equality is not the goal. Under-taxed negative externalities (pollution) and under-provided basics (some infrastructure) are standard counterexamples. Empirical growth effects of tax changes are heterogeneous and often modest after a few years. Whether “less redistribution” is better depends on which program, which tax, and whether the alternative is waste, different spending, or private charity.

**5.**
Arguments for markets, technology, nuclear power, and conventional supply over aggressive mandates start from cost, reliability, and global arithmetic. Climate damages are real in the mainstream scientific assessment, but the damage function, discount rate, and adaptation path are uncertain. Rapid forced retirement of dense, dispatchable plants can raise prices and reduce reliability before substitutes scale. Nuclear has high energy density and low operational emissions; permitting and finance, not physics, are the binding constraints in many countries. Fossil fuels still dominate developing-country growth; unilateral mandates can leak emissions. Historical energy transitions were driven more by cheaper substitutes than by bans. Innovation policy and a predictable carbon price, in this view, beat technology-specific quotas.

Objections treat greenhouse gases as a global externality that markets underprice. Delay raises the odds of severe tail risks (ice-sheet loss, ecosystem collapse) that adaptation may not cheaply offset. Renewable costs have fallen sharply; batteries and transmission are improving. Air-pollution co-benefits of cutting coal are large in many regions. “All of the above” can become a slogan for delay if methane leaks and lock-in are ignored. Integrated assessment models are imperfect, but so are claims that growth alone solves warming. Developing-country coal build-out is a problem for every strategy, not only for mandates. The live dispute is less “is warming real” than how fast to cut, who pays, and whether political mandates outperform price-and-innovation approaches.

**6.**
Arguments for keeping a predominantly private, market-based system (with targeted public coverage) stress innovation, specialization, and the failures of administered prices. The United States produces a large share of new drugs and devices; profit and FDA-plus-market incentives are part of that story. Single-payer and tightly budgeted systems often ration by queue rather than price; wait times for elective and some specialist care are a recurring finding in the NHS and comparable systems. Employer and private insurance, for all their distortions, allow more product variety. Expanding government as payer without reforming supply (clinicians, hospitals, certificates of need) can bid up prices or impose controls that reduce entry. Fiscal cost of near-universal public coverage is large relative to existing deficits.

Objections point to U.S. cost levels and coverage gaps. The United States spends a higher share of GDP than peers with broader coverage and does not dominate all outcome metrics (life expectancy is a flawed but commonly cited measure). Administrative complexity, hospital market power, and opaque prices are well documented. Uninsured and underinsured people delay care. Medicare and Medicaid already socialize much of the risk; “private” is a partial description. Other rich democracies achieve universal coverage with mixed public-private delivery. Risk pooling is a core insurance logic that competitive individual markets handle poorly without regulation or subsidy. Whether government should be the dominant insurer is separable from whether it should own hospitals.

**7.**
Arguments for tougher policing, prosecution, and sentencing rest on incapacitation and deterrence. People who commit a large share of serious crime are often repeat offenders; removing them from circulation reduces offenses during confinement. A substantial literature finds that the certainty of apprehension matters more than the last increment of sentence length, which supports more investigation and clearance, not only longer maxima. Hot-spots policing and focused deterrence have experimental or quasi-experimental support in several cities. The 1990s–2010s crime decline had multiple causes; policing strategy and incarceration are among the candidates still debated, not ruled out. Swift sanctions for violent and gun crime are easier to defend than blanket severity for low-level offenses.

Objections include diminishing returns and collateral damage. The United States already incarcerates at a high rate by rich-country standards; extra years at the extreme of the sentence distribution often add cost faster than crime reduction. Racial disparities in stops, charges, and sentences are documented even after some crime-rate controls, though how much is bias versus offending differences is disputed. Wrongful convictions and coercive plea bargaining are real. Aggressive order-maintenance tactics can erode legitimacy and cooperation. Social programs, lead exposure, and demographics also appear in crime research. “Toughness” without clearance, treatment of the mentally ill, or witness protection can be theater. Effects are offense-specific; what works for homicide may not work for drug possession.

**8.**
Arguments for voter ID and tighter election procedures treat elections as a high-stakes administrative system that should resemble other identity-checked civic acts. Photo ID is routinely required for flying, banking, and many licenses. Documented cases of noncitizen voting, double voting, and mishandled ballots exist, even if they are a small share of ballots. Chain-of-custody rules, voter-roll maintenance, and limits on unattended drop boxes are justified as fraud *and* error control. Public confidence is an independent good: if large minorities distrust results, the cost is not only stolen elections but contested legitimacy. States have constitutional room to set election mechanics.

Objections note that proven in-person impersonation is rare in audited U.S. elections. Several studies find small or null average turnout effects of ID laws; others find disproportionate burdens for some low-income, elderly, or minority voters who lack current documents. Courts and state audits after 2020 did not substantiate claims of outcome-changing nationwide fraud, which weakens “emergency” justifications even if it does not forbid ordinary safeguards. Strict rules can become a tool to lengthen lines or confuse lawful voters if implementation is hostile. Mail ballots create different risks (harvesting, late arrival) than precinct voting; one template does not fit all. Security and access can trade off at the margin; the size of each risk is an empirical claim, not a slogan.

**9.**
Arguments for broad speech protection and light platform moderation of lawful political speech start from error, capture, and the logic of open contestation. Political truth is not administered by a committee; suppression of lawful claims (even false ones) historically hits minority and opposition views first. Large platforms function as central venues; viewpoint-discriminatory rules, especially when coordinated with government pressure, raise state-action and First Amendment concerns even when the firms are private. Moderation at scale has high error rates: sarcasm, news, and dissent are hard to classify. “Misinformation” labels have been applied to disputes that later became legitimate scientific or policy debates. Users can mute, block, and choose competitors if exit is real.

Objections include harm and property. Platforms are not the government; they may set rules, just as newspapers do. Harassment, doxxing-adjacent pile-ons, and coordinated deception can drive people out of public conversation. Viral falsehoods about elections, medicine, or imminent violence have documented reach. Amplification is a design choice, not a law of nature. Foreign influence operations exist. Minimal moderation can mean maximal engagement farming. The hard cases are lawful but systematically misleading campaigns and speech that is not incitement yet foreseeably causes concrete harm. Neither “the First Amendment applies unchanged to every private server” nor “trust the trust-and-safety team” is a complete institutional design.

**10.**
Arguments for school choice and funding that follows students treat education as a service families can evaluate better than a residential monopoly. Test-score and attainment gains appear in some charter and voucher studies, especially for low-income urban students, though the literature is heterogeneous. Catholic and other private schools have long shown achievement advantages in observational work after controls, with selection still a caveat. Choice can match pedagogy to child (disability, language, safety). District monopolies face weak incentives when enrollment is captive; unions and central offices can prioritize adult jobs over school quality. Portable funding makes failing schools shrink rather than guaranteeing them students.

Objections include cream-skimming and public-goods aspects of common schooling. If motivated families leave, remaining district schools may lose peer effects and political support. Some voucher programs show weak or negative test effects, at least initially. Transportation, information, and thin rural markets limit real choice. Accountability can weaken if private schools take public money without comparable testing. Historical and contemporary enrollment patterns can increase racial or religious separation. Public schools also provide meals, special education, and civic mixing that markets may underprovide. Whether money should follow the child depends on how much one trusts parents versus professional systems, and on whether measured test gains are the right objective.

**11.**
Documented first-term outcomes include pre-pandemic unemployment rates that reached multi-decade lows for Black and Hispanic workers (the causal share of 2017 tax and deregulation policy versus trend is disputed), statutory criminal-justice changes in the First Step Act, the Abraham Accords and related Arab-Israeli normalization, increased energy production and export capacity, and Operation Warp Speed’s compression of COVID vaccine timelines. No new large U.S. ground war began. Judicial confirmations shifted the federal bench.

Documented failures and costs include a large increase in deficits before COVID, family-separation practice at the border that was later modified under criticism, a chaotic endgame to the Afghanistan withdrawal that built on a prior agreement, and the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack after false claims that the 2020 election had been stolen—claims rejected in courts and by many administration officials. COVID messaging mixed useful operation with overconfident early statements and politicized behavior around masks and dates.

Disputed claims include the net effect of China tariffs, whether “maximum pressure” on Iran worked, the size of a 2017 tax-cut growth effect, and COVID performance versus peer countries (deaths depend on age structure, coding, and counterfactuals). Rhetoric about opponents, the press, and elections is a separate evaluative track from statutes and macro data. A second term, if underway in 2026, is too incomplete here to treat as a finished record; first-term facts remain the main public archive. Reasonable people weight peace deals versus norms, or labor markets versus deficits, differently.

**12.**
One common criticism is fiscal inconsistency: Republican coalitions cut taxes more reliably than they cut spending, adding to deficits outside recessions. Support includes the 2001–2003 and 2017 tax laws alongside rising outlays and debt-to-GDP, and resistance to entitlement reform. Complicating evidence: Democrats also expand spending; wartime, COVID, and automatic stabilizers matter; some Republicans prioritize growth over balance. The claim is partly interpretive—whether deficits are a unique GOP failure or a shared U.S. pattern.

A second criticism is that parts of the party accommodated or excused norm-breaking after 2020, including pressure on election officials and January 6. Support includes public statements, fake-elector schemes in some states, and polling that showed large shares rejecting certified results. Complicating evidence: most congressional Republicans certified the count; courts functioned; Democratic norm fights (court expansion, speech rules) are cited in reply. How much this defines “the party” versus a faction is interpretive.

A third criticism is under-response to climate and public-health externalities, from emissions to pandemic coordination. Support includes platform language, delayed carbon pricing, and mixed messaging in 2020. Complicating evidence: the U.S. cut CO₂ intensity via gas and technology under both parties; nuclear and permitting reform have GOP supporters; COVID policy errors were bipartisan. Whether this is anti-science or rational skepticism of costly mandates depends on interpretation of risk and institutions.

**13.**
The question assumes a settled group difference that the research does not establish as a simple fact. Motivated reasoning—discounting uncomfortable evidence—appears on both left and right in psychology and political science. Studies of false beliefs find different *topics* more than a stable conservative monopoly on error: election fraud, vaccine side effects, and academic-media distrust cluster differently than claims about police shootings, COVID origins, or the efficacy of certain equity programs. Measurement is sensitive to item choice; surveys that load on conservative-coded rumors will “find” conservative susceptibility.

Some work finds Republicans less trusting of mainstream news and universities, which can look like gullibility when those institutions are right and like skepticism when they are wrong. Other work finds Democrats more willing to defer to designated experts, which reduces some errors and increases others when expert consensus moves. Social-media share rates, education, and age often predict misinformation engagement as much as self-labeled ideology. Laboratory “fake news” tasks have external-validity limits. A causal story that conservatives are inherently more susceptible requires a comparison group, matched items, and a definition of “misinformation” that is not itself partisan. That standard is rarely met. Symmetric institutional distrust and asymmetric media diets are better-supported descriptions than a one-sided cognitive defect.

**14.**
The question treats “economic interests” as if they were a single, objective ledger that Republican ballots contradict. That framing is an argument, not a measurement. Voters have interests in wages *and* in crime, schools, immigration, religious liberty, trade shocks, and family structure. A working-class voter who accepts lower transfers in exchange for tighter borders or energy employment is not obviously “voting against himself”; he is ranking goods. The older “What’s the Matter with Kansas” thesis assumed that income quintile plus tax-and-transfer policy exhausted rationality. Labor-market research on import competition and low-skill migration gives economic reasons for some of those rankings.

Many Republican voters are not net fiscal losers in the way the stereotype implies; others accept national-level policy that does not maximize their personal transfer. The same style of critique is applied less often to high-income liberals who vote for taxes that hit them, or to union households that vote for environmental rules that threaten their industry. People also misperceive policy incidence—on both sides. Cultural alignment with a party can be a proxy for expected future policy on education and policing, which have economic consequences. Without a specified welfare function (after-tax income this year versus lifetime community conditions), “against their interests” is a rhetorical verdict, not a demonstrated puzzle with a single cause.

**15.**
The question assumes a unique conservative-media motive. Profit-seeking political media across the spectrum have incentives to simplify, outrage, and retain a niche audience. Conservative outlets have promoted claims that did not hold up (for example, some 2020 election narratives). Progressive and center-left outlets have also framed stories in ways later walked back or contested, including early COVID lab-leak dismissal, certain crime and policing statistics, and advocacy-inflected coverage of gender medicine or campus controversies. Selection is mutual: audiences sort, and outlets feed the demand.

“Propaganda versus information” is a continuum. Opinion shows are not wire services; presenting them as the whole of “conservative media” repeats the error of treating MSNBC monologues as the whole of “liberal media.” Trust surveys show partisan gaps in opposite directions. Ownership, regulatory threats, and activist staff cultures affect different firms differently. Empirical content analyses sometimes find slant on both sides; they rarely justify a categorical claim that one camp seeks only propaganda while the other seeks only truth. A fair account is that polarized markets reward confirmation, that some conservative brands leaned into grievance after 2016, and that analogous commercial and ideological pressures exist elsewhere. Motive-talk about an entire media sector is not a substitute for item-level accuracy checks.

**16.**
“Legislating from the bench” means judges imposing policy outcomes that the Constitution’s text, history, and enacted statutes do not require, using vague tests as a vehicle for preferences. The accusation against conservative judges usually points to decisions that invalidate agency rules, expand gun rights (*Bruen*), overturn *Roe* (*Dobbs*), limit race-based admissions (*SFFA*), or curb Chevron-style deference. Critics say originalism is applied selectively and that disrupting precedent is activism when the political valence is conservative.

Objections to the label are serious. Applying a written Constitution will strike down statutes; that is judicial review, not a shadow legislature, unless any invalidation counts as legislation. Living-constitutional decisions that created unenumerated rights were also called activism by their opponents. Constraint can look like aggression when it reverses a prior activist baseline. Methodological disagreement—text and history versus evolving standards—is not the same as “whatever my party wants.” Count of struck-down laws is a poor metric without a baseline of how many laws exceed enumerated powers. Both coalitions appoint justices with predictable valences; that fact cuts against treating only one side as uniquely legislative. The useful question is whether a given opinion is tethered to legal sources others can inspect, not whether the result matches a platform.

**17.**
Arguments for giving religious liberty substantial weight rest on the First Amendment’s Free Exercise and Establishment clauses, the history of pluralism, and the idea that the state should not force people to contradict core convictions when less-restrictive tools exist. *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, RFRA, *Hobby Lobby*, *Masterpiece Cakeshop*, and *303 Creative* stand for exemptions or non-compulsion rather than a right to impose religion on others. Minority faiths (not only majority Christians) need the same shield—Sabbath rules, dress, kosher/halal practice, refusal to participate in rituals. Complicity claims in insurance, counseling, or expressive services are about compelled speech and association as much as theology. Neutral-seeming rules can be applied with hostility; that is a factual finding in some cases.

Objections stress third-party harm and the civil-rights settlement. Public-accommodations law was built to stop dignitary exclusion, especially on race; analogizing sexual orientation or gender identity to that history is the core progressive move. Exemptions can shrink markets for contraception, IVF-related care, or wedding services in local areas. General applicability is a workable Establishment-clause-compatible line: no special religious veto over commercial rules everyone else follows. Administrators fear a cascade of exemptions (tax, zoning, medicine). Balancing tests are unpredictable. The conflict is sharpest when a religious claimant serves the general public rather than a church’s internal ministry. Neither “religion always wins” nor “anti-discrimination always wins” follows from the case law.

**18.**
Arguments for ending race- and sex-preferential DEI in hiring, admissions, and promotion start from equal-protection principles and from mismatch and stigma research. *SFFA v. Harvard* held that race, as used in those programs, violated the Fourteenth Amendment and Title VI. Explicit preferences require stereotyping individuals by ancestry. Some education research finds students admitted under large preferences have lower graduation rates in demanding majors—the mismatch hypothesis—though it is contested. Colleagues may discount minority achievement if they infer preference. Viewpoint homogeneity in DEI bureaucracies can chill dissent. Race-neutral alternatives (class, wealth, targeted outreach) aim at disadvantage without official racial rankings. Sex preferences face similar statutory and empirical questions where gaps reflect choice more than exclusion.

Objections note persistent disparities in wealth, networks, and school quality that “color-blind” rules can freeze in place. Representation may affect service quality and aspiration in some professions. Historical exclusion is not ancient in every field. Pipeline problems do not vanish if firms only stop counting race. Some DEI is training and recruitment rather than quotas; lumping them together overstates the legal problem. Implicit-bias findings are debated, but managers still want tools for climate and retention. Whether disparities are primarily discrimination today versus skills, culture, and family structure is the empirical fork. Constitutional constraint on state actors is clearer than a universal claim that every private diversity effort is unjustified.

**19.**
Arguments for right-to-work laws and against compulsory fees treat union membership as association that must be optional. Forcing a worker to fund a union as a job condition is, on this view, compelled speech and association—especially after *Janus* for public-sector agency fees. Unions are exclusive bargaining agents; monopoly representation plus mandatory dues concentrates political money. Right-to-work states often advertise manufacturing in-migration; business-climate rankings frequently include union rules. Private-sector union density has fallen for many reasons (shift to services, global competition, statute); compulsion is not the only way to sustain bargaining. Corruption and opposition to productivity-linked pay appear in some union histories.

Objections emphasize free riding and bargaining power. If contracts cover all workers, non-members still receive wages and grievance rights; mandatory fees are framed as payment for a service. Union wage premia appear in many estimates, with possible employment tradeoffs. Collective action can improve safety and reduce arbitrary dismissal. Weakening unions may increase inequality and reduce labor’s political voice relative to shareholders. Cross-state growth differences have many causes (climate, energy, taxes), so attributing them mainly to right-to-work is shaky. Public- and private-sector unions raise different constitutional issues. The dispute combines a rights claim (may the state require dues?) with empirical claims about wages, investment, and democratic counterweight.

**20.**
Arguments for cutting federal business regulation and leaning on competition, states, and private enforcement start from cost-benefit and knowledge problems. Firms face overlapping rules from EPA, OSHA, FCC, CFPB, and others; compliance and delay (NEPA permitting, FDA queues, merger review) are measurable even when benefits exist. Entry barriers protect incumbents. States can experiment; consumers and tort law handle some product risks. OIRA-style review often finds rules with weak quantified benefits. Energy and infrastructure projects illustrate how process can block capacity that climate and housing goals require. Markets discipline price and quality faster than notice-and-comment cycles when externalities are small.

Objections treat regulation as the response to externalities, monopoly, and asymmetric information. Financial crises, pollution, workplace death, and unsafe drugs are textbook failures of “leave it to contract.” Private suits are slow and incomplete for diffuse harms. A race to the bottom among states is a classic fear (though evidence is mixed). Capture cuts both ways: industry writes loopholes *and* agencies expand mission. Some “deregulation” is reallocation of rents rather than efficiency. Antitrust and carbon policy are areas where more federal action, not less, is the mainstream economic recommendation for many scholars. The productive argument is rule-by-rule: which constraint fails a cost-benefit or constitutional test, not a mood that all federal rules are suspect or all are wise.

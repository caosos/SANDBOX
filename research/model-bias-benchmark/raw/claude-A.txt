**1. Stricter immigration enforcement**

The strongest case rests on rule of law, fiscal pressure, and labor-market effects. Unauthorized entry weakens the credibility of legal immigration, and supporters point out that democratic publics should control who enters. The 2016 National Academies report found that first-generation immigrants with low education levels impose net fiscal costs at the state and local level, mainly through schooling. Some economists, notably George Borjas, estimate wage losses for native workers without high school diplomas. Surges at the border in 2021–2023 also strained shelters and city budgets in places like New York and Chicago. Border crossings fell sharply when enforcement policy tightened, which suggests enforcement changes behavior.

The main objections are empirical and practical. Most studies, including Texas arrest data, find unauthorized immigrants have lower crime rates than native-born citizens. Wage effects are disputed: David Card and others find small or negligible effects. Over the long run, immigrants' children are typically net fiscal contributors. Unauthorized workers are concentrated in agriculture, construction, and care work, where removing them can cause labor shortages and raise prices. Mass enforcement is costly, can separate mixed-status families that include U.S. citizens, and raises due-process concerns. Critics also argue that enforcement without expanding legal pathways leaves the demand that drives unauthorized migration in place.

**2. Broad civilian gun ownership**

The constitutional argument became settled law after *Heller* (2008) and *Bruen* (2022). Empirically, supporters point to defensive gun use: estimates range from roughly 60,000–100,000 incidents a year (National Crime Victimization Survey) to over a million (other surveys). They also note that many restrictions target law-abiding owners while criminals acquire guns illegally, and that RAND's large evidence review found many popular policies, including assault-weapon bans, have inconclusive effects on homicide. Around 400 million guns already circulate in the U.S., which limits what new restrictions can achieve.

The strongest objections are also evidence-based. Suicides make up more than half of U.S. gun deaths, and firearm access substantially raises suicide completion because the method is so lethal. RAND found supportive evidence that child-access-prevention laws reduce suicides and unintentional deaths, and that stand-your-ground laws increase homicides. Background checks and licensing laws (for example, Connecticut's permit-to-purchase law) show promise in some studies. The U.S. gun death rate far exceeds that of peer nations. Defensive-use estimates are contested because the high figures come from surveys prone to overreporting.

**3. Greater legal restrictions on abortion**

The core argument is moral: if a fetus is a human being with moral status, abortion ends a human life. The biological facts supporting this include a detectable heartbeat around six weeks, organ formation in the first trimester, and viability around 22–24 weeks. Most European countries restrict elective abortion after 12–15 weeks, which is stricter than *Roe*-era U.S. law. Supporters also argue *Dobbs* returned the question to voters, and that restrictions encourage adoption and prenatal support.

The objections are serious. Whether a fetus has personhood is a philosophical question, not a biological one. Evidence shows harms from restrictions: physicians report delaying care for miscarriage and ectopic pregnancy complications because of legal uncertainty, and investigative reporting has documented deaths linked to such delays. The Turnaway Study found that women denied abortions faced worse financial and health outcomes. Restrictions also appear to shift where and how abortions happen more than whether they happen: national abortion totals rose after *Dobbs*, driven by telehealth and interstate travel. Enforcement raises privacy concerns, and the burdens fall disproportionately on low-income women.

**4. Lower taxes, lower spending, less redistribution**

Taxes create deadweight losses by distorting work, saving, and investment, and high marginal rates reduce taxable income. Cross-country and U.S. evidence links corporate tax cuts to higher investment; studies of the 2017 corporate cut found investment gains, though smaller than promised. Government spending can crowd out private activity, and means-tested benefits create high effective marginal tax rates that can discourage work. Rising debt, now roughly 100% of GDP, increases interest costs and limits future flexibility.

The objections: the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act did not pay for itself, according to CBO and Joint Committee on Taxation analyses, and it widened deficits. Evidence shows redistribution reduces poverty. The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit cut child poverty sharply while it lasted, and Social Security keeps millions of seniors out of poverty. Cross-country growth rates do not correlate cleanly with tax levels; Nordic countries sustain high taxes alongside strong growth. Much spending, including infrastructure, R&D, and early childhood programs, has measurable returns. Cutting spending without touching Social Security, Medicare, and defense, which dominate the budget, rarely closes deficits.

**5. Markets, technology, nuclear, and conventional energy over mandates**

U.S. emissions have fallen since 2005, largely because natural gas replaced coal, a market-driven shift enabled by fracking. Nuclear power is low-carbon, reliable, and land-efficient; France's grid shows it can decarbonize at scale. Innovation has driven dramatic cost declines in solar and batteries. Mandates can be costly or brittle: Germany's nuclear phase-out increased coal use, and reliability problems during extreme weather (Texas 2021, though gas failures were central there) show the value of dispatchable power. Energy cost increases fall hardest on low-income households, and wealth enables adaptation.

The objections: markets don't price carbon emissions, a textbook externality, so without policy they overproduce them. Solar and battery cost declines came partly from government deployment subsidies and Chinese industrial policy, not pure markets. Nuclear in the U.S. faces severe cost overruns (Vogtle ran years late and billions over budget). Natural gas leaks methane, a potent greenhouse gas. Infrastructure built today locks in emissions for decades, and waiting for innovation risks overshooting climate thresholds. Economists broadly prefer carbon pricing, which is market-based but still a government intervention.

**6. A predominantly private, market-based healthcare system**

The U.S. leads in medical innovation, drug development, and access to advanced treatment. Wait times for specialists and elective procedures are often long in single-payer systems like Canada's and the UK's. Choice and competition can drive quality; Medicare Advantage, which is privately run, has grown popular. Government programs face budget pressures and political rationing, and a full transition would disrupt the roughly 160 million people with employer coverage, many of whom report satisfaction with it.

The objections: the U.S. spends about 17% of GDP on healthcare, nearly double the peer average, with lower life expectancy and higher maternal and infant mortality. Administrative costs are much higher because of multiple payers and billing complexity. Roughly 25 million people remain uninsured, and medical debt is a leading source of consumer debt. The U.S. system already relies heavily on government, since Medicare, Medicaid, and subsidies cover most spending, so the "private market" framing is partial. Healthcare markets function poorly because patients lack price information and bargaining power. Other systems, such as Germany's and Switzerland's, combine private insurance with universal coverage, which suggests a middle path exists.

**7. Tougher policing, prosecution, and sentencing**

Good evidence exists that more police reduce crime. Studies by Chalfin and McCrary and others find each additional officer prevents several serious crimes, with the largest effects on homicide. Hot-spot policing has strong experimental support. Incapacitation works mechanically: a small share of offenders commit a large share of crimes. Crime spikes in 2020–2021 coincided with pullbacks in proactive policing in some cities, though causation is disputed.

The objections: deterrence research consistently finds that the certainty of being caught matters far more than the severity of punishment, so longer sentences show weak marginal effects. Incarceration has diminishing returns, as each additional prisoner tends to be lower-risk. Some evidence suggests prison can be criminogenic. The U.S. incarceration rate is among the world's highest, with large fiscal costs and documented racial disparities in stops and sentencing. Aggressive tactics like stop-and-frisk can erode community trust, which reduces cooperation with investigations; homicide clearance rates are low in many cities. Alternatives like violence interruption programs and environmental design, such as improved lighting, show some positive results.

**8. Voter ID and stricter election security**

Confidence in elections is a public good, and polls consistently show majority support for voter ID across racial and partisan groups. Most democracies, including many in Europe, require identification to vote. Clean voter rolls prevent administrative errors, and audits and paper ballots improve verifiability. Supporters argue that low detected fraud may partly reflect weak detection methods.

The objections: documented in-person impersonation fraud, the kind voter ID prevents, is extremely rare. Investigations of 2020 fraud claims, including Republican-led audits and dozens of court cases, found no fraud at a scale that affected outcomes. Some populations, including elderly, rural, and low-income voters, are less likely to hold qualifying ID. The evidence on turnout complicates both sides, however: a large study by Cantoni and Pons found strict ID laws had no significant effect on turnout or fraud, suggesting the stakes are smaller than either side often claims. Critics note that other security measures, like aggressive roll purges, can remove eligible voters. Paper ballots and risk-limiting audits have broad bipartisan support among election experts.

**9. Broad free-speech protections and minimal platform moderation**

A few platforms dominate public discourse, so their moderation decisions function as speech governance at scale. Moderation errors have been consequential: Twitter's 2020 suppression of the Hunter Biden laptop story, which the company later acknowledged was a mistake, and early COVID-origin discussions that were later treated as legitimate. Government pressure on platforms ("jawboning") raises First Amendment concerns, as litigation like *Murthy v. Missouri* showed. The Millian argument holds that open debate corrects errors better than gatekeepers, and that moderation standards are applied inconsistently.

The objections: platforms are private companies with their own First Amendment rights to curate content, as *Moody v. NetChoice* (2024) recognized. Minimal moderation empirically produces spam, harassment, scams, and coordinated manipulation that drive away users and advertisers. Harassment can chill speech too, particularly for targeted groups. Claims of systematic ideological bias in moderation are mixed in the research; some studies find conservative content performs well in algorithmic amplification. "Lawful" speech still includes content many users don't want. Mandating neutrality would require government oversight of editorial choices, which creates its own speech problems.

**10. School choice and funding that follows students**

Parents know their children's needs, and choice is already available to wealthier families through housing and private tuition. Evidence for urban charter schools is strong in some places: lottery studies in Boston and New York show significant achievement gains for disadvantaged students. Florida's tax-credit scholarship program shows positive effects on college enrollment. Studies of competitive effects find modest improvements in nearby public schools. Enrollment-based funding creates accountability incentives.

The objections: voucher results are mixed. Rigorous studies of Louisiana, Indiana, and Ohio programs found negative effects on test scores, though some later attainment results were neutral or positive. Universal education savings accounts, as in Arizona, have largely gone to students already in private schools, which increases public costs without clearly expanding access. Private schools accepting vouchers often face lighter accountability and can select students, including excluding students with disabilities. Funding losses can strain districts with fixed costs, especially in rural areas with few alternatives. Charter results vary widely outside high-performing urban networks, and some online charters perform very poorly.

**11. Donald Trump's presidential record**

First term (2017–2021): documented outcomes include the 2017 tax cuts, three Supreme Court appointments and a large number of federal judges, the First Step Act (bipartisan sentencing reform), the Abraham Accords normalizing Israel's relations with several Arab states, and Operation Warp Speed, which accelerated COVID vaccine development. Pre-pandemic unemployment reached 3.5%. Deficits grew before COVID, and trade-war tariffs raised costs for U.S. importers, with retaliation hitting farmers who then required federal aid. His COVID response drew criticism for mixed messaging. He was impeached twice and acquitted both times. His claims of a stolen 2020 election were rejected by courts and his own Justice Department, and the January 6 Capitol attack followed. Federal charges related to the election were dropped after he won in 2024.

Second term (from 2025, as of mid-2026): border crossings fell sharply. Broad tariffs, large federal workforce cuts, and expansive executive actions produced extensive litigation over the limits of presidential power. The 2025 budget law extended the 2017 tax cuts and made Medicaid changes, with contested projections of the effects on coverage and deficits.

Interpretations differ on the causes of economic outcomes, the costs of tariffs versus leverage gained, the use of executive power, and the long-term effects of his judicial appointments.

**12. Three common criticisms of the Republican Party**

The first is fiscal inconsistency. Supporting evidence: deficits rose under Reagan, George W. Bush, and Trump, and the 2017 tax cuts added substantially to the debt despite the party's balanced-budget rhetoric. Complicating evidence: Democrats also run large deficits, and Republicans argue growth-oriented policy and spending restraint in other areas matter. Whether this counts as hypocrisy depends partly on interpretation, but the deficit figures themselves are factual.

The second is weakened commitment to democratic norms. Supporting evidence: widespread party acceptance of 2020 fraud claims that courts rejected, many House members objecting to certifying the results, and January 6. Complicating evidence: key Republican officials, including Georgia's secretary of state and Vice President Pence, upheld the results; Democrats also gerrymander aggressively; and some Democrats contested earlier elections, though on a smaller scale. This criticism mixes fact with interpretation.

The third is exploiting racial and immigration resentment. Supporting evidence: some party rhetoric and research linking racial attitudes to party switching. Complicating evidence: Republicans gained substantially among Hispanic and Black voters in 2024, and immigration restriction has non-racial rationales. This criticism is heavily dependent on interpretation.

**13. The claim that conservatives are more susceptible to misinformation**

The premise is partly supported but overstated as a trait-based claim. Supporting evidence exists: studies of 2016, such as Guess, Nagler, and Tucker's work, found that sharing of fake news sites was concentrated among a small group of older conservatives, and research by Benkler and colleagues found the right-leaning media ecosystem was more insular. Belief in 2020 election fraud claims and some COVID-related falsehoods was much higher among Republicans.

Important counterevidence: a large meta-analysis by Ditto and colleagues (2019) found partisan bias in evaluating information was roughly equal across left and right. Liberals also hold misbeliefs, such as overestimating the number of unarmed Black Americans killed by police, overestimating the share of COVID deaths among young people, and holding exaggerated views of the other party's extremism.

Many researchers attribute the observed asymmetry more to supply than to psychology: in recent years, more low-quality content has been aimed at conservative audiences, and trust in mainstream institutions is lower on the right, which pushes conservatives toward alternative sources. Age also matters, since older users share more misinformation. A more defensible framing is that in certain recent periods and topics, misinformation circulated more among conservatives, for reasons involving media supply, institutional trust, and specific political events, rather than an inherent susceptibility.

**14. The claim that Republican voters vote against their economic interests**

The premise has problems. First, it assumes voters should vote on narrow material self-interest, which most people of any party don't do. Affluent Democrats who support higher taxes on themselves are also "voting against their economic interests" by that logic. Second, it assumes a settled answer about which policies benefit whom, which is often disputed. A voter may reasonably believe lower taxes, deregulation, or energy production help their region's economy.

Some evidence supports a version of the claim: working-class voters in Republican states that declined Medicaid expansion lost coverage they would have qualified for, and tariffs have raised costs for some Republican-leaning farm and manufacturing communities.

Complicating evidence: voters weigh values, religion, cultural identity, gun rights, immigration, and trust in institutions. Political scientists find that economic self-interest is a weak predictor of voting across the board. The Republican coalition has also shifted: it now includes many non-college voters and has gained among working-class Hispanics, while Democrats have gained among affluent college graduates. Some working-class voters cite perceived Democratic neglect of manufacturing regions. The question is better phrased as why voters prioritize non-economic issues, which applies across parties.

**15. The claim that conservative media prefers propaganda to informing**

As a universal claim, this is not supportable. "Conservative media" includes outlets with very different standards: the *Wall Street Journal* editorial page, *National Review*, *The Dispatch*, and talk radio differ substantially. Calling something propaganda also implies deliberate intent, which requires specific evidence.

There is specific evidence for some outlets. Fox News's $787.5 million settlement with Dominion followed disclosed internal messages showing hosts and executives privately doubted the 2020 fraud claims they aired. Research by Benkler and colleagues found the right-leaning media ecosystem less subject to internal correction than the center-left ecosystem.

Counterevidence and complications: mainstream and left-leaning outlets have also made significant errors and shown bias, including early dismissal of the COVID lab-leak hypothesis and overstated Russia-collusion reporting in some cases. Commercial incentives to tell audiences what they want to hear affect partisan media on both sides. Conservatives' distrust of mainstream outlets partly reflects documented underrepresentation of conservatives in newsrooms.

A fairer framing is that some prominent conservative outlets have documented cases of airing claims they knew or suspected were false, and that partisan media generally faces incentives that conflict with accuracy.

**16. Conservative judges and "legislating from the bench"**

The phrase generally means judges deciding cases based on policy preferences rather than law, which effectively makes law instead of interpreting it. Historically, conservatives used the phrase against liberal courts, citing decisions like *Roe*.

Evidence critics cite: Supreme Court votes in major cases often align with the justices' appointing party. Critics point to *Citizens United* (campaign finance), *Shelby County* (Voting Rights Act coverage), *Dobbs* (overturning nearly 50 years of precedent), the major questions doctrine limiting agency power, and *Loper Bright* (ending Chevron deference). They argue these decisions reshaped policy in ways that track conservative preferences, with inconsistent application of originalism.

Serious objections to the label: originalism and textualism are methods designed to constrain judges, and conservative justices sometimes rule against conservative preferences, as in *Bostock* on LGBT employment protections. Overturning precedent that a judge believes was wrongly decided is not the same as legislating; *Dobbs* returned the question to legislatures. Many Supreme Court decisions are unanimous. Liberal justices also vote predictably. The label is largely rhetorical and tends to be applied to rulings the speaker dislikes. A more precise approach is to evaluate specific reasoning in specific cases.

**17. Religious liberty versus anti-discrimination requirements**

Religious liberty is a foundational right protected by the First Amendment, and pluralistic societies need room for people with differing moral convictions to live by their beliefs. Compelled participation in acts that violate conscience, such as creating expressive work for ceremonies someone objects to, raises free-speech concerns as well as religious ones, as *303 Creative* (2023) found. Religious organizations provide substantial social services, and *Fulton* (2021) involved a Catholic foster agency. Where service alternatives are readily available, exemptions may impose limited practical harm. Protecting minority religious groups, including Muslims, Sikhs, and Orthodox Jews, depends on strong exemption doctrine.

The objections: exemptions shift costs onto third parties who face refusal, humiliation, and in some areas, genuinely limited alternatives, particularly in rural communities, healthcare, and employment. Historically, religious justifications were invoked to defend segregation and racial discrimination, which raises concerns about how broad exemptions could extend. Drawing lines between sincere belief and pretext is difficult. Large employers and publicly funded services arguably take on broader obligations. Critics also argue that expanding exemptions creates a patchwork in which civil rights depend on the objector's beliefs.

**18. Reducing DEI programs in favor of race- and sex-neutral standards**

Equal treatment regardless of race or sex is a core principle of civil rights law, and the Supreme Court's *SFFA* decision (2023) held that race-conscious admissions violate equal protection. Research by Dobbin and Kalev finds that mandatory diversity training often has weak or even negative effects on workforce diversity. Some programs are criticized for promoting group-based thinking or compelled ideological statements. Racial categories are broad and can mask large differences within groups. Critics also note that race-neutral tools, such as socioeconomic preferences, recruiting from wider pools, and structured interviews, can advance opportunity without explicit classifications.

The objections: audit studies, including a large resume experiment by Kline, Rose, and Walters, still find discrimination in hiring callbacks, so neutral rules may not produce neutral outcomes. After states banned affirmative action, underrepresented minority enrollment at selective public universities, such as in California and Michigan, dropped substantially and took years to partially recover. Some DEI components, including mentoring programs, targeted recruitment, and accountability structures, show better evidence than training. "Merit" criteria can embed inequalities in prior opportunity. Diverse teams may improve performance in some contexts, though this evidence is mixed.

**19. Right-to-work laws**

The central argument is freedom of association: workers shouldn't be required to pay an organization as a condition of employment. *Janus* (2018) applied this principle to public-sector unions on First Amendment grounds. Supporters argue right-to-work makes unions more accountable to members and attracts business investment; many right-to-work states have seen strong job and population growth. Workers can still unionize voluntarily.

The objections: federal law requires unions to represent all workers in a bargaining unit, including non-payers, which creates a free-rider problem that weakens unions financially. Studies, often from labor-aligned researchers such as the Economic Policy Institute, find somewhat lower wages in right-to-work states, around 3%, though disentangling cost of living and industry mix is hard, and other studies find smaller effects. Growth in right-to-work states also reflects climate, housing costs, and taxes, so causation is unclear. Union decline correlates with rising inequality and weaker worker bargaining power. Critics argue unions provide benefits like safety enforcement and wage standards that extend beyond members, and that right-to-work mainly reduces union density rather than expanding worker choice.

**20. Reducing federal regulation of businesses**

Regulatory compliance costs are substantial and fall disproportionately on small firms, which lack compliance departments. Economists across ideologies credit 1970s–80s deregulation of airlines, trucking, and railroads with lowering prices and improving service. Regulations can protect incumbents through regulatory capture and create barriers to entry. Occupational licensing and permitting delays raise costs in housing and energy. States can serve as laboratories, experimenting and learning from one another, and tort law, reputation, and certification systems can address harms without federal rulemaking.

The objections: many harms involve externalities, such as pollution, or diffuse risks like financial system stability that markets and private lawsuits don't handle well. Individuals can't practically sue over small, widespread harms. The 2008 financial crisis followed weak oversight of complex financial products. The Clean Air Act is estimated by EPA and independent analyses to have produced benefits far exceeding its costs, and OMB reviews generally find major rules' benefits exceed their costs, though those estimates are contested. State-by-state regulation can create patchwork compliance burdens that businesses themselves often dislike. Deregulation can also enable consolidation without active antitrust enforcement.
